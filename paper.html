Abstract—With the increasing influx of long-form digital video into the education, corporate, and media sectors, intelligent retrieval systems that go beyond conventional keyword search are required. In this paper, we introduce NeuroClip, a full-stack video processing platform that turns static video archives into dynamic, queryable knowledge bases. NeuroClip fuses two information modalities—ASR transcripts produced by AssemblyAI and visual text obtained from sampled frames via EasyOCR—and enriches sentence-level units with dense 384-dimensional semantic vectors derived from the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer. To strengthen frame–text alignment for educational content, we additionally train a custom dual-encoder model (ResNet-50 visual encoder + DistilBERT text encoder) on the MSR-VTT dataset using a symmetric InfoNCE objective; the model attains Recall@1 = 0.527 and Recall@10 = 0.918 after five epochs. A windowed retrieval pipeline followed by Cross-Encoder re-ranking selects and merges the most contextually relevant transcript segments, after which FFmpeg extracts and H.265/HEVC compresses the playable clips. A job-keyed persistent caching architecture eliminates redundant re-processing and yields sub-second cached query latency. Experimental evaluation across 50 structured query cases and a 15-case per-step pipeline benchmark demonstrates strong semantic retrieval precision (Precision@3 = 0.82, nDCG@3 = 0.84) with the two-stage cross-encoder pipeline, while H.265/HEVC compression achieves 72.1–77.8% file-size reduction. The system is deployed as a PWA with a React/TypeScript frontend, FastAPI/Python backend, and vector storage managed by Supabase (PostgreSQL + pgvector).

Index Terms—semantic video retrieval, sentence embeddings, multimodal fusion, contrastive learning, dual encoder, ASR, OCR, vector search, cross-encoder re-ranking, video compression.

I.  Introduction

Video is the dominant medium of knowledge dissemination across educational platforms, corporate intranets, and social networks. Industry traffic reports estimate that video accounts for more than two-thirds of all consumer Internet traffic, and platforms such as YouTube, Coursera, and enterprise video repositories already contain billions of hours of recorded content [1]. Despite this abundance, locating a specific concept within a long-form video remains fundamentally inadequate with current tools [2].

Traditional video search solutions attempt to address this by metadata tagging or by performing naïve keyword matching against automatically generated transcripts. These approaches exhibit three well-known failure modes: (i) a semantic gap—keywords fail to capture user intent; (ii) fragmentation—retrieved snippets lack surrounding context; and (iii) computational redundancy—the same video is re-processed for every search query.

NeuroClip addresses all three failure modes through a unified, intent-aware pipeline. Its key contributions are:

  • (1) Multimodal fusion of ASR speech transcripts with frame-level visual OCR text into a single sentence-aligned representation.
  • (2) A custom dual-encoder model (ResNet-50 + DistilBERT) trained with symmetric InfoNCE on MSR-VTT for educational frame–text alignment, achieving Recall@10 = 0.918.
  • (3) Dense vector semantic search using pre-computed 384-dimensional sentence embeddings for fast candidate retrieval.
  • (4) Windowed retrieval with Cross-Encoder re-ranking for coherent, context-rich clips.
  • (5) Job-keyed persistent caching that eliminates redundant transcription, OCR, and embedding work across queries.
  • (6) Codec-aware H.265/HEVC compression for lightweight delivery of the extracted clips.
II.  Related Work
A. Semantic Sentence Embeddings

Reimers and Gurevych [3] proposed Sentence-BERT (SBERT), a Siamese and triplet network architecture built on pre-trained BERT to produce semantically meaningful fixed-size sentence embeddings. Before SBERT, computing pairwise semantic similarity for 10,000 sentences required approximately 65 hours; SBERT reduced this to roughly 5 seconds. NeuroClip employs the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 distillation of SBERT as its primary text encoder.

B. Cross-Encoder Re-ranking

Nogueira and Cho [4] demonstrated that a BERT-based Cross-Encoder significantly outperforms BM25 and Bi-Encoder baselines on MS MARCO when the query and candidate passage are jointly encoded. NeuroClip adopts the same two-stage design—fast Bi-Encoder candidate selection followed by Cross-Encoder re-ranking on a short candidate list—balancing precision and latency.

C. Transformer Architecture Foundations

Vaswani et al. [5] introduced the Transformer architecture, based entirely on self-attention without recurrence or convolution. Both the ASR models and the sentence-embedding models used in NeuroClip ultimately derive from this design.

D. Vision–Language Contrastive Learning

Radford et al. [6] introduced CLIP, demonstrating that aligning image and text representations through a contrastive objective scales remarkably well across visual recognition tasks. Inspired by this paradigm, our custom frame–text encoder uses a ResNet-50 visual backbone and a DistilBERT text backbone, optimised with a symmetric InfoNCE loss [7] on MSR-VTT [8] to specialise the encoder for educational video content.

E. Video Summarization and Retrieval

Apostolidis et al. [9] survey deep-learning approaches to video summarization, identifying key-frame selection, temporal segmentation, and attention-based highlight detection as dominant paradigms; NeuroClip departs from these by adopting a query-conditioned retrieval strategy rather than unsupervised highlight extraction. Saini et al. [13] and Kadam et al. [14] further analyse recent video-summarization techniques and the open challenges of generalising across domains. Yang et al. [10] proposed Vid2Seq for dense video captioning, and Sun et al. [11] proposed VideoBERT for self-supervised video representation. These systems target captioning rather than playable-clip retrieval.

III.  System Architecture and Methodology
A. Overview

NeuroClip is a full-stack Progressive Web Application (PWA) with a modular processing pipeline. The system accepts video input through three API endpoints: local file upload, URL-based download via yt-dlp, and a combined ingestion-and-query endpoint. Upon receipt, a unique job identifier (job_id) is generated using UUID4, serving as the primary key that links every processing artifact throughout the system lifecycle. Fig. 1 summarises the end-to-end pipeline, organised into three modules: (A) multimodal indexing and summarization, (B) two-stage retrieval and clip construction, and (C) clip extraction and codec-aware compression.

Fig. 1.  NeuroClip end-to-end architecture: multimodal indexing (ASR + OCR + sentence embeddings), two-stage retrieval (Bi-Encoder + Cross-Encoder), and codec-aware clip compression.
B. Automated Speech Recognition (ASR)

Audio is extracted from each ingested video and submitted to the AssemblyAI transcription API, which returns an SRT-format transcript with word-level timestamps. The SRT output is parsed into sentence-level units, each carrying text, start_time, and end_time fields. AssemblyAI was selected for its superior accuracy on conversational and lecture-style audio compared with locally hosted Whisper models. On our 15-case per-step benchmark (Section IV.B), the transcription stage attains F-Score = 80.00%, Precision = 100.00%, and Recall = 66.67%.

C. OCR-Based Visual Signal Enrichment

Frames are sampled at a fixed interval (default: every 3 s) to capture on-screen information such as slide text, whiteboard equations, and code snippets. Each frame is processed by EasyOCR, and recognised text regions are merged with the nearest transcript sentence by timestamp proximity, enabling semantic matching of search queries against screen-displayed content. To strengthen frame–text alignment for educational footage we additionally fine-tune a custom dual-encoder visual–text model (Section III.G) on MSR-VTT [8]. On the 15-case benchmark, the EasyOCR stage attains F-Score = 69.57%, Precision = 100.00%, and Recall = 53.33%.

D. Sentence-Level Embedding Generation

Each enriched sentence unit (speech text + merged OCR context) is encoded into a 384-dimensional dense vector using the sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, selected for its strong trade-off between embedding quality, CPU-friendly inference speed, and a memory footprint of roughly 80 MB. Sentences are processed in batches of 32. We denote the embedding of a sentence si by ei ∈ ℝ384. On the 15-case benchmark, the vectorization stage attains F-Score = 33.33%, Precision = 30.00%, and Recall = 37.50%, indicating that a generic sentence encoder is the bottleneck, motivating the custom dual-encoder of Section III.G.

E. Retrieval Pipeline
1) Query embedding:

The user's natural-language query q is encoded in the same 384-dimensional space using the identical all-MiniLM-L6-v2 encoder, ensuring semantic alignment between query and document representations.

2) Windowed retrieval:

Rather than retrieving individual sentences, NeuroClip uses a windowed retrieval scheme. A window Wk of w consecutive sentences is treated as a single retrieval unit and represented by mean pooling of its constituent embeddings,

eWk = (1/w) ∑i∈Wk ei,
(1)

and ranked by cosine similarity against the query embedding,

sim(q, Wk) = (eq · eWk) / (‖eq‖·‖eWk‖).
(2)

Configurable parameters include window_size = 5, stride = 2, min_clip_duration = 10 s, and max_clip_duration = 120 s. On the 15-case benchmark this stage attains F-Score = 80.00%, Precision = 100.00%, and Recall = 66.67%.

3) Cross-Encoder re-ranking:

The top-N candidate windows are submitted to cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2. The query and the concatenated window text are jointly encoded by a single Transformer; the relevance score is taken from the [CLS] token,

r(q, Wk) = φCLS([q ‖ text(Wk)]),
(3)

and replaces the initial cosine similarity. If the re-ranker is unavailable, the system gracefully falls back to embedding-only ranking.

4) Neighbour merging:

Adjacent highly-scored windows are merged into contiguous temporal segments: two windows are merged if their temporal gap is below a configurable threshold (default: 2 s), preventing multiple overlapping clips of the same discussion thread.

F. Clip Extraction and Codec-Aware Compression

Each merged segment is materialised by FFmpeg as a playable MP4 clip with a ±1.5 s temporal pad. The compression module then performs codec-aware post-processing using the H.265 / HEVC standard [12]: hevc_nvenc is preferred when an NVIDIA GPU is available, with CPU libx265 as a fallback. The output resolution is capped at 1280×720 by default. As reported in Section V.F, this configuration achieves 72.1–77.8% file-size reduction across the tested profiles while maintaining perceptually acceptable quality for educational content delivery.

G. Custom Dual-Encoder for Frame–Text Alignment

Off-the-shelf sentence encoders capture text-only semantics and ignore the visual structure of educational frames (slides, code, diagrams). To close this gap we train a custom dual-encoder model that produces image and text embeddings in a shared 256-dimensional metric space. The architecture consists of a ResNet-50 visual backbone followed by a learnable linear projection,

f(v) = Wimg · ResNet50(v) ∈ ℝ256,
(4)

and a DistilBERT text branch using the [CLS] token,

g(t) = Wtxt · DistilBERT(t)[CLS] ∈ ℝ256.
(5)

Both embeddings are L2-normalised so that cosine similarity reduces to an inner product,

f̂(v) = f(v) / ‖f(v)‖2,  ĝ(t) = g(t) / ‖g(t)‖2.
(6)

For a mini-batch of N aligned pairs we compute the similarity matrix Sij = f̂(vi)·ĝ(tj) and minimise the symmetric InfoNCE objective [7],

ℒimg = −(1/N) ∑i log [exp(Sii/τ) / ∑j exp(Sij/τ)],
(7)
ℒ = (ℒimg + ℒtxt) / 2,  τ = 0.07,
(8)

where ℒtxt is symmetric to (7) with rows and columns of S swapped. Optimisation uses AdamW (lr = 1×10−4, weight decay = 1×10−4, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999) with a cosine learning-rate schedule (ηmin = 1×10−6) over 5 epochs, batch size 32, on a single NVIDIA Tesla P100 (16 GB) on Kaggle. The MSR-VTT dataset [8] (179,994 train / 20,000 validation captions) provides the supervision. The trained encoder is integrated into NeuroClip as an OCR-augmented re-ranking signal that boosts retrieval relevance for visually rich educational frames; quantitative training results are reported in Section V.D.

IV.  Experimental Evaluation
A. Experimental Setup

All experiments and model training were conducted on a combination of a local workstation (Intel Core i7, 16 GB RAM, NVIDIA GTX 1650) and Kaggle cloud infrastructure with NVIDIA Tesla P100 (16 GB) and dual Tesla T4 (2 × 16 GB) GPUs. The software stack was Python 3.10, FastAPI, sentence-transformers 2.2.2, assemblyai 0.17.0, easyocr 1.7.0, torch 2.0.1 (CUDA 11.8), torchvision 0.15, transformers 4.31, FFmpeg 6.0, and Supabase PostgreSQL 15 with pgvector 0.5.0.

B. Evaluation Datasets

Two datasets were used. (i) Retrieval benchmark: a structured query benchmark of 50 query–video cases spanning 10 academic domains—Computer Science (16), Artificial Intelligence (8), Mathematics (8), Physics (3), Cybersecurity (3), Economics (3), Biology (3), Chemistry (2), Engineering (2), and Statistics (2). Ground-truth segment boundaries were labelled by human annotators on a three-point relevance scale. A complementary 15-case per-step pipeline benchmark records true-positive (TP), false-positive (FP), and false-negative (FN) counts at every stage. (ii) Dual-encoder training: the MSR-VTT dataset [8] (199,994 captions over ≈10,000 video clips) was split into 179,994 training and 20,000 validation captions to train the custom ResNet-50 + DistilBERT encoder of Section III.G. Validation of the slide-extraction pipeline on a representative 1-hour educational lecture yielded 107 unique extracted slides, of which 104 were verified text-rich, giving an estimated precision of 97.2%.

C. Retrieval Quality Metrics

Standard information-retrieval metrics were used at K = 3 and K = 5: Precision@K, Recall@K, nDCG@K, Temporal IoU, and Mean Boundary Error (MBE) in seconds. The Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank K is

DCG@K = ∑i=1K (2reli−1) / log2(i+1),
(9)

and the normalised version is nDCG@K = DCG@K / IDCG@K, where IDCG@K is the DCG of the ideal ranking. Per-step pipeline metrics (F-Score, Precision, Recall) were computed from TP, FP, and FN counts against the annotated ground truth.

C. Temporal Accuracy

The system achieves a Mean Temporal IoU of 0.74, with a Mean Boundary Error of 2.1 s (start) and 2.8 s (end). The ±1.5 s temporal padding applied during clip extraction contributes to boundary error but improves subjective clip quality by avoiding abrupt cuts. Removing the padding improves MBE to 1.3 s / 1.9 s at the cost of perceptibly clipped content.

VI.  Limitations

ASR dependency. Retrieval quality is bounded by transcription accuracy. Videos with heavy background noise, strong accents, or domain-specific jargon exhibit the most significant degradation, as reflected in the 66.67% recall of the transcription stage on the 15-case benchmark (Table V).

Frame-extraction sensitivity. The frame-extraction stage attains only F-Score = 33.33% (Precision = 30.00%, Recall = 37.50%), indicating that a fixed 3 s sampling interval misses relevant frames in fast-paced or slide-transition-heavy content.

Generic sentence encoder. Vectorisation with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 attains only F-Score = 33.33% on the per-step benchmark; this directly motivates the custom dual-encoder of Section III.G, but at present the dual encoder is integrated only as a re-ranking signal rather than as the primary retrieval index.

Evaluation scale. The 50-query retrieval benchmark and the 15-case per-step pipeline benchmark are limited in scale. Larger evaluations (500+ query–video pairs) are needed to fully characterise performance across domains.

Multilingual support. The current configuration is optimised for English; multilingual content requires model reconfiguration and increases processing overhead.

VII.  Future Work

Scaling the dual encoder. Extending dual-encoder training to 1 M+ video–caption pairs and replacing the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embeddings as the primary index (rather than only a re-ranking signal) is the most direct path to closing the 33.33% F-Score gap of the current vectorisation stage.

Visual-semantic embedding. Adding OpenCLIP or BLIP-2 features alongside the dual encoder would let queries retrieve clips based on visual events beyond what the transcript captures, further addressing the OCR recall limitation.

Query rewriting with LLMs. A lightweight query-expansion module using Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) would improve recall for vague or under-specified queries by generating multiple semantically equivalent rewrites.

Adaptive frame sampling. Replacing fixed 3 s sampling with scene-change detection would lift frame-extraction recall from 37.50% by targeting visually distinct keyframes rather than uniform samples.

Real-time processing. Incremental transcript and embedding updates during recording would enable live-lecture search with sub-minute indexing lag.

Benchmark expansion. A large-scale, publicly available evaluation benchmark for educational video retrieval—analogous to MS MARCO for passage retrieval—would significantly advance comparative research in this area.

VIII.  Conclusion

We have shown that a single, modular Progressive Web Application can deliver semantically rich, computationally efficient retrieval over long-form educational video using only open-source components. The two key engineering observations are: (i) fusing ASR transcripts with OCR-derived visual text at the sentence level meaningfully reduces the semantic gap between user queries and video content, raising Precision@3 from 0.71 (embedding-only) to 0.82 once Cross-Encoder re-ranking is added; and (ii) job-keyed persistent caching is sufficient to amortise the dominant ingestion cost, yielding 0.6–0.8 s cached query latency irrespective of video length. The two key research observations are: (iii) generic sentence encoders are the dominant bottleneck for educational frames (F-Score = 33.33% at the vectorisation stage), and (iv) a small, dedicated ResNet-50 + DistilBERT dual encoder trained for only five epochs on MSR-VTT recovers Recall@10 = 0.918 for frame–text alignment, confirming that targeted contrastive fine-tuning is the most cost-effective lever for further gains. Coupled with codec-aware H.265/HEVC compression (72.1–77.8% file-size reduction), NeuroClip is immediately applicable to educational content navigation, corporate knowledge management, and media production.
